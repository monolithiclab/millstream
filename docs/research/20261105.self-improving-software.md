# Self-improving software: analysis

Checked 2026-11-05 against Boris Tane, "Self-improving software is inevitable", 2026-10-10. Millstream facts come from
this repo's spec, roadmap and code at commit `4c592f5`. This note compares and proposes; each proposal needs its own
decision before it reaches the spec.

Status, 2026-11-05: proposals 1 and 2 are built, so gap 2 and the `distill` sensor below describe the code as this note
found it. With audits on, the M5 day costs 5.2% of production's, against the 3.7% quoted below.

## Summary

The essay says agents have automated the forward pass of software (intent to code) and left the backward pass
(production signal to code) to people, who carry data between tools by hand. Software should read its own signals,
change itself and check the result. People move to defining what "better" means.

Millstream is that loop, built for one kind of software: pipelines that mix code, agents and people. It holds what the
essay still has to assemble: signals in one log, labels from reviewers, attribution through lineage, replay over
history. It also has what the essay barely mentions: proof before a change ships. A backtest, a holdout the drafting
agent never saw, and base evals that a proposal cannot rewrite.

Read against its own code, the loop still fails the essay's main test in five places. The most serious is cheap to fix:
**with default settings, neither a `lessons` nor a `distill` proposal starts on its own**, and a person carries the
evidence from production to development by hand. "Pipelines that learn" is true on request, and not yet true unattended.

The strategy that follows: stay narrow, compete on proof, make the loop run unattended up to the point where a person
accepts, keep it measuring after it succeeds, and let autonomy grow only from measured results.

## What the article argues

- **People are the feedback loop.** Each signal has its own tool. Someone correlates them, writes a ticket and
  prioritises it. A "meat proxy" is a person whose only job is to carry information between two machines that could talk
  directly. Connectors make the proxy faster, and the person stays in the middle.
- **Automations have an unknown unknowns problem.** Alert-triggered agents see only what someone decided was worth
  seeing, and inherit every blind spot of what triggers them.
- **Applications need backpropagation.** Code is the weights, signals are the output. The gradient is computed by hand
  at a planning session, from memory and whichever complaints were loudest.
- **Intelligence is now too cheap to meter**, so it can read every trace and ticket and join them.
- **People define the fitness functions.** Each function brings its own, and they pull against each other. Choosing the
  weights and the hard constraints, precisely enough for a machine, "was always the hard part".
- **It is close enough to build.** Two scheduled agent prompts with read access to every signal and write access to the
  repo, opening pull requests with the evidence attached.
- **To get there, "we have to give up being in charge".**

The author says he is building a product on this thesis (Polylane), so the essay is also a pitch.

## Where the essay is right about Millstream

Each gap was checked in the code on the date above.

**1. Someone still carries the data.** `millstream evidence export` and `evidence import` are manual, with no schedule
and no pull. Imported insights are listed but not recorded, so they fire no trigger (`m5-exit.md`, "Not covered").
D31's split between production and development is sound. Only the carrying is the proxy, and the essay's definition
describes it exactly.

**2. The default configuration filters out the loop's headline proposals.** `improve.minSeverity` defaults to `warning`,
for the insight trigger and the weekly run alike (`engine/insight.go`, `improve/gather.go`). `distillable` is always
`info` (`insights/distill.go`). `reviewPattern`, which carries the `lessons`, `topology` and `autoApprove` hints, is
`info` unless sampled-rule agreement falls under 90% (`insights/review.go`). The M5 exit runs `millstream improve
--insight` by hand, so its success says nothing about this path. Setting `improve.minSeverity: info` works around it.
Severity measures how bad a problem is, and an opportunity is neither bad nor urgent, so severity is the wrong filter
for these two kinds.

**3. Some insights lead nowhere.** `ideasOf` (`improve/gather.go`) makes ideas from `reviewPattern`, `failureCluster`,
`slowStep` and `distillable`, and from lessons files near their limit. `waste`, `cost`, `drift` and `regression` make
none. So a `drift` raised by an audit stops at the insight, although section 11.5 said `improve` proposes a fix or a
rollback; the spec is corrected in the same change as this note.

**4. The loop only sees what its kinds describe.** A new kind of failure that matches none of the nine stays invisible
to `improve`. Distribution drift (section 11.1) is unbuilt, and that is the kind that would catch silent degradation
when nobody reviews the items.

**5. Nothing checks a proposal after it ships.** A validated proposal records `proposalApplied`, and no one compares the
production figures afterwards with what the backtest predicted. An insight may resolve on its own, but nothing links
that to the proposal. The essay's closing step, "check whether it worked", stops at release.

## A risk the essay does not name: the loop removes its own sensors

Every success reduces the signal the next improvement depends on. `distill` removes the agent's answers and its review
agreement; `autoApprove` removes reviewers; a filter moved upstream removes the items people would have rejected.
Millstream has the right guard, and applies it unevenly:

- `autoApprove` is done properly. `sampleRate` defaults to 0.05 with a minimum of 0.01, "so the rule is always measured"
  (`pipeline/review.go`), and sampled agreement under 90% raises a warning.
- `distill` is not. Its patch writes `fallback: { action, when: [abstain] }` with no `auditRate`
  (`improve/distill.go`, `ROADMAP.md`), so a distilled step keeps no check against the agent unless someone adds one.
- Reviewer agreement is itself a proxy: a downstream outcome (the lead that closed, the report a customer returned) has
  no way back into the item's history.

A self-improving pipeline therefore needs a rule, which `sampleRate` already embodies: a change that removes a signal
must leave a sampled one in its place.

## Where Millstream goes further than the essay

The essay's backpropagation has no validation set. Training on production signal and shipping the result, with a pull
request as the only check, is how a model overfits. Its example prompts end at "open a PR with the fix". Millstream's
strongest material is exactly what is missing there:

- **A holdout by time and entity**, served by the engine and never visible to the drafting agent (section 11.5).
- **Base evals decide.** A proposal that changes evals is judged by the old ones, so it cannot redefine its own success.
  This is the guard against Goodhart's law that fitness functions need, and it should be named as such.
- **Deterministic detection.** Insights are computed with no model, so they cost nothing, reproduce exactly and work
  with no API key. A model drafts; the engine judges.
- **Untrusted signal is treated as untrusted.** The essay's setup gives one agent read access to every support ticket
  and write access to the repo. A ticket can carry instructions. Millstream fences evidence, confines patches per kind,
  separates forge credentials from candidate code and publishes only the validated hash (section 11.7, D33).
- **Personal data.** Reading every session and ticket is a data protection question. Evidence bundles redact, expire and
  stay findable by erasure (section 11.2).
- **Intelligence is metered, and the loop's best results use less of it.** Inference may be cheap enough for the
  backward pass, sampled and budgeted. On the forward path it is still paid per item, it varies between runs and it
  slows every step. `distill` is a self-improvement that removes the model, which the essay's framing cannot express.
  The M5 scenario cut a day's agent cost to 3.7%, on synthetic listings whose answers follow a rule; real data has yet
  to give a figure.

## "Give up being in charge"

The essay's own examples end in pull requests, and the author's product page says its fixes "go through your normal
review and CI" (read 2026-11-05), so a person still merges. The real distinction is what the person does, and it has
three stages:

1. **Proxy:** finds the problem, carries the data, describes it. This is what the essay rightly wants gone.
2. **Judge:** reads a validated diff and its report, and accepts or rejects it. Millstream today.
3. **Policy author:** decides which classes of change need no judge, and on what evidence. Parked in D29.

Stage 3 has a template already built and tested. `review.autoApprove` with `sampleRate` lets a rule approve while people
review a sample, and a falling sampled agreement raises an insight. The same mechanism applies to proposals: a class
earns auto-acceptance through its measured record, and loses it the same way. That record does not exist until gap 5 is
closed, so the order is fixed: measure first, then delegate. Accountability (D29) stays intact, because a person wrote
the policy and can read what it did.

## What it means for the strategy

1. **Stay narrow. Item pipelines are where this loop works first.** Application-level loops need connectors to every
   signal tool, attribution across systems, and judgement on changes that cannot be replayed. Millstream has discrete
   items, labels from reviewers, immutable lineage and replayable history. Competing on general application
   self-improvement would give all of that up. An application team can still run such a loop *on* Millstream, as a
   pipeline with budgets, review and transcripts: that is a use case to show, and no reason to widen the engine.
2. **The label will get crowded, so compete on proof.** "Self-improving" will soon be claimed by many tools, and most
   will mean an agent that opens pull requests. Millstream's claim to defend is the one that is hard to copy: every
   change is proven on history and data the drafter never saw, by a judge that is not a model.
3. **Make the claim true by default before M6.** An open-source release will be read against "pipelines that learn". A
   first user who waits a month for a proposal, because of a severity default, will conclude that it does not. This
   moves proposals 1 and 2 below ahead of the release.
4. **Real evidence is the next milestone that matters.** Every figure published so far comes from synthetic listings.
   The first pilot should record the intervention rate and the cost per approved item over time. Nothing else would
   prove that the loop works.
5. **Keep the human position, and word it as a sequence.** Saying "a person approves everything" forever reads as a
   limitation next to this essay. "Autonomy is earned per class of change, from a measured record" answers it, and it
   suits buyers who must answer for a change.

## Proposals

Ranked by value against cost. None is committed; the items are parked in `ROADMAP.md`, each needing a decision.

1. **Start `improve` on opportunities.** Treat a `distillable` insight and a `reviewPattern` with proposal hints as
   actionable whatever their severity, with `improve.minSeverity` still governing problems. Small; a behaviour change,
   no contract change. Do before M6.
2. **Audit what `distill` replaces by default.** The patch sets `auditRate` when both actions declare `effects: none`,
   from a new `improve.distill.auditRate` (default 0.02), as `sampleRate` already does for `autoApprove`. Small; do
   before M6.
3. **Carry evidence without a person.** A scheduled export in production to a location the deployment names, and a pull
   in development; imported insights fire `insight` triggers once, keyed by bundle and insight. Same redaction, expiry
   and export record, so D31 holds. Medium; already parked for the pull half.
4. **Measure after release.** For each applied proposal, compare the insight's figures and the pipeline's cost per
   approved item and intervention rate over a window before and after, against the backtest's prediction, and record it
   on the proposal. A miss raises an insight, and a clear regression drafts a rollback. Later drafts read the record.
   Medium; it needs a decision on windows and attribution when several proposals land together. Prerequisite for 8.
5. **Declared objectives.** A pipeline key stating targets and hard constraints over the metrics Millstream already
   measures, such as cost per approved item at most X, or false drops at most 1%. Insights report the gap, proposals are
   ranked by it, and validation fails on a hard constraint. This is the essay's fitness function, written where its
   owner can review it. It changes the pipeline file, a public contract, so it needs a spec change. Medium to large. It
   must stay measures over thresholds in `filter` steps: objectives judge a pipeline and never route an item (section
   4.7).
6. **A discovery step in `improve`.** A budgeted agent reads an evidence sample with no insight to start from and states
   hypotheses, each as a query the engine can run (CEL over items and deliveries). Only what the engine confirms becomes
   an insight of a new kind, `finding`. This addresses the unknown unknowns while the engine still holds no LLM (D30).
   Medium; its false-positive cost is unknown, so try it behind a key.
7. **Outcome signals.** A way to attach a late label to an item by id or key, such as "this lead closed", kept with the
   item and read as truth by backtests and `distill`, alongside reviewer edits. Today's labels stop at the review.
   Large; it changes the API and evidence, so it needs a real pilot asking for it.
8. **Policy-based acceptance.** D29's parked item, on the `autoApprove` and `sampleRate` model, per proposal kind, in
   development only, after 4 has produced a record. Large.
9. **Measure the machinery.** Per kind and insight: proposals drafted, accepted and rejected, cost per accepted
   proposal, realised against predicted effect. Report it before considering anything recursive. Small once 4 exists.

Smaller corrections: make `waste` draft the `topology` proposal it already suggests in words, and `drift` draft a fix or
a rollback (gap 3). A use-case page and example package for an application feedback loop run as a pipeline, once the
example has really been run.

## What not to build, and why

- **Learning live in production.** It breaks revision hashes and replay (D29, D31). The tension to relieve is the
  carrying of data between deployments (proposal 3), and the separation itself stays.
- **A model in the engine's detection or judgement.** Insights and validation stay deterministic (D30). Discovery lives
  in the `improve` package, and the engine verifies what it finds.
- **Connectors to every signal tool in the core.** A signal source is an action or a webhook trigger, like any other. A
  connector catalogue is a commercial-edition concern (D11), and the engine's advantage does not depend on one.
- **`improve` improving itself automatically.** A pipeline never feeds itself, on purpose: a loop that rewrites its own
  drafting and validation can drift toward whatever passes. A person can already fork the package; proposal 9 gives that
  person the numbers.
- **One blended fitness score.** The essay shows why: the goals conflict, and a single number hides the choice.
  Objectives stay separate, with hard constraints stated as such.
